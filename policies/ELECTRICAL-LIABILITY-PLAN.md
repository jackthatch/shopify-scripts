## 1. NRTL listing - the position

There is **no federal law saying you must have a UL mark to sell a light fixture**. That is
true and it is misleading. In practice:

- **OSHA 29 CFR 1910.303(a)** requires electrical equipment used in workplaces to be
  "approved", meaning tested and certified by a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory (NRTL).
- The **National Electrical Code** requires equipment to be listed and used in accordance with
  its listing. **An unlisted fixture fails an electrical inspection in virtually every US
  jurisdiction** - so a customer who hired an electrician, or who has a permit, finds out.
- **UL and ETL are legally equivalent.** Both are OSHA-recognised NRTLs testing to the same
  standards. For lighting: **UL 1598** (luminaires), **UL 153** (portable lamps),
  **UL 8750** (LED drivers). Intertek (ETL), CSA and TUV are equally acceptable.

**The CE mark means nothing in the United States.** It is a self-declaration of European
conformity, not a third-party listing. Importing CE-only lighting without an NRTL mark can
cause customs delays or rejection.

**Verify, do not trust a logo.** Counterfeit UL marks on imported goods are common.
- **UL Product iQ** - search by company, model number, or UL file number (the "E-number")
- **Intertek ETL directory** - same logic
- **CSA product listing database**

Check the model is listed, the listing is **active**, and which standard it was tested against.
**A UL 8750 component listing is not a UL 1598 luminaire listing** - a driver being recognised
does not mean the assembled fixture was evaluated.

## 2. CPSC Section 15(b) - the duty you cannot avoid

This is the sharpest exposure and it applies to **retailers**, not just manufacturers.

- Federal law requires manufacturers, importers, distributors **and retailers** to report to
  CPSC **immediately, generally within 24 hours**, after obtaining information reasonably
  supporting the conclusion that a product contains a defect which could create a substantial
  product hazard, or creates an **unreasonable risk of serious injury or death** (16 CFR 1115).
- The duty arises **from the first information** - complaints, injury reports, QC data. The
  regulation says a firm **should not await complete or accurate risk estimates before
  reporting.**
- If you investigate, the 24-hour clock starts when you have information supporting the
  conclusion, and the Commission treats **10 days** as the outer limit of a reasonable
  investigation.
- **GE paid a $3.5 million civil penalty** for failure to report defective products in time.

**Practically:** one customer email saying "the lamp sparked and scorched the wall" starts a
legal clock on you. Not the second email. The first. Have a written process before you launch:
log the date received, preserve the product and photos, do not argue with the customer, and
report if the threshold is met.

## 3. California Proposition 65

- Enforcement is **private**: any person may file suit, the plaintiff keeps **25% of penalties**,
  and penalties run to **$2,500 per day per violation**. This drives systematic "bounty hunter"
  litigation against consumer goods.
- **Electrical cords are a primary target.** Prop 65 limits lead in PVC cord insulation to
  **200 ppm - about 3x stricter than federal CPSIA limits.** A cord that passes federal
  requirements can still require a warning.
- **Businesses with fewer than 10 employees are exempt** from the warning requirement. As a
  solo operator you are likely below that threshold. **But**: the threshold is 10 employees
  worldwide, the exemption does not cover indemnity obligations imposed by retailers or
  marketplaces, and it disappears the moment you grow.
- Warning rules tightened: warnings must now **name at least one specific chemical**, and by
  1 January 2028 all short-form warnings must.

**Practically:** add a compliant Prop 65 warning to the electrical listings anyway. It is cheap
and it is the standard mitigation.

## 4. Insurance

Typical US market rates:

- **Product liability for a small e-commerce seller:** roughly $300-$2,000/yr, with many small
  sellers paying about $500-$1,000
- **Importer product liability:** roughly $700-$3,000/yr
- **GL + product liability + product recall, imported goods:** about $150-$250/month
- Average product liability claim against a small business runs around **$25,000** - and that
  is an ordinary claim, not a house fire

Ask specifically whether **imported electrical goods are covered** and whether **product recall**
is included - many policies quietly exclude both.

## The candid recommendation

The **hardwired fixtures are the real problem**, not the plug-in lamps. A ceiling light, a
chandelier, a wall sconce - these get installed permanently, often by an electrician who will
notice a missing listing, and they are the products most likely to be implicated in a fire.

Either source genuinely listed hardwired fixtures, or do not sell hardwired fixtures at all and
concentrate on plug-in table and floor lamps. That is a hard commercial call, but it is the one
that changes your actual risk position rather than your paperwork.
